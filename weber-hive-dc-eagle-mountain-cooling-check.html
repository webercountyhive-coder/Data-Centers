"Zero Water Lost"? What the Records Say About Eagle Mountain's Data Center Cooling

A reader comment says the data centers being built in Eagle Mountain use closed-loop cooling and lose no water to evaporation. Part of that matches the record. Part of it leaves things out.

Posted Oct. 28, 2026 · Updated Oct. 28, 2026

The short version

  • QTS, the campus going up in Eagle Mountain now, says it uses closed-loop cooling that "would only need to be filled once." That is the company's own claim; no independent numbers have been published.
  • Not every Eagle Mountain data center works that way. Meta's campus there withdrew more than 35 million gallons in 2024, and a 2018 city agreement keeps its monthly water use confidential.
  • "Zero water" covers the cooling loop only. The buildings still use city water, and experts say the larger share of a data center's water use is usually at the power plants that make its electricity.
  • Utah's new water-reporting law (H.B. 76) only covers data centers that open on or after July 1, 2026 and pass a 75 acre-foot yearly line.

Where the water still goes

A closed loop takes evaporation out of the cooling step. It doesn't take water out of the whole picture.

To be fair to the comment: it's right that closed-loop cooling is a real improvement over evaporative cooling, and that it costs more energy to run. Novva's CEO estimated his campus would use about 250 million gallons a year with evaporative cooling, compared with about 3 million with his system. If QTS's numbers hold up, that is a meaningful difference.

Will anyone be able to check?

Utah's new law, H.B. 76 (primary sponsor Rep. Jill Koford), requires data centers of 10,000 square feet or more to report their water use to the state, which posts the numbers. It covers only centers that begin operating on or after July 1, 2026, and only those that withdraw 75 acre-feet a year or more (about 24.4 million gallons). Reports are due Sept. 1 each year.

  • QTS is expected to open after July 1, 2026. If its yearly withdrawal reaches the 75 acre-foot line, its numbers will be public. If its closed loop keeps it below that line, it won't have to report.
  • Meta opened before July 1, 2026, so H.B. 76 doesn't require it to report. Whether new buildings at an older campus count as "new" is not settled in the records reviewed.

Rep. Koford told KUTV the bill is "a start." Full details: our H.B. 76 page.

Questions to ask, and to whom

  • Ask: Eagle Mountain City Council, public comment at a council meeting — How many gallons did the QTS closed-loop system take for its first fill, and what yearly water use is written into the QTS development agreement?
  • Ask: Eagle Mountain City Council — Will the city make Meta's monthly water use public, now that the campus has grown since the 2018 agreement?
  • Ask: Utah Division of Water Rights — Will the QTS Eagle Mountain campus file an H.B. 76 report, and when will its numbers be posted?
  • Ask: Legislature's Economic Development and Workforce Services Interim Committee, public comment — Do new buildings at campuses that opened before July 1, 2026 count as "new" data centers under H.B. 76?
  • Ask: Utah Public Service Commission, public comment on Rocky Mountain Power filings — How much new electricity will the Eagle Mountain campuses draw, and from which power plants?

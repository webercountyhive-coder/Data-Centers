Roy Prop 10: Voters Are Asked to Keep or Reject a $1.75 Million Tax Increase

Roy's City Council adopted a 34.25% property-tax increase on Aug. 11, 2026. Residents are gathering signatures right now for a referendum on it, and Weber County's certified ballot already lists it as Proposition #10. Here is what the council adopted and why, what a yes or no vote does, and where the petition stands.

Referendum
A citizen petition that puts a local government's decision on the ballot so voters can keep it or reverse it.
Certified Tax Rate
The rate that brings in about the same property-tax money as last year, not counting new buildings. Anything above it is an increase.

Utah's required notice and public hearing before a city can adopt a rate above its certified rate.

New Here? The Short Version

Roy first proposed raising its share of property taxes by 54.94%. After cuts, the council adopted a 34.25% increase on Aug. 11, 2026, by a 4-1 vote, mainly to pay for employee raises. Residents started a referendum to let voters decide. Weber County's certified Nov. 3 ballot lists it as Proposition #10. A yes vote keeps the increase. A no vote sends Roy's rate back to its most recent certified rate. Signature gathering is still under way as of Oct. 1, 2026, so whether the petition gets enough valid signatures is still open.

1. What the Council Adopted, and Why

Roy posted its Truth in Taxation notice on July 23, 2026. It proposed a 54.94% increase, raising $2,807,745 more a year. On a $439,000 home, Roy's share of the bill would have gone from $388.25 to $601.69. The public hearing was held Aug. 11, 2026, at 6 p.m. at 5051 S. 1900 W.

According to the city's agenda packet, the money pays for three things: covering a shortfall from an employee cost-of-living raise given in March 2026, a 2.8% cost-of-living raise for employees in fiscal year 2027, and wage-correction adjustments for all full-time employees.

At the hearing, staff said cuts, including savings tied to the proposed fire annexation, brought the increase down to about 34.29%. Removing that raise brought it to about 34.25%. Councilmember Wilson then moved to approve Resolution 26-24 “with a tax rate of 34.24%,” striking the raise for the council and mayor while keeping the employee raises. Why the ballot says $1,749,050 instead of a percent State law gives the exact wording for a property-tax referendum: it must state the dollar amount of new revenue. $1,749,050 divided by Roy's current budgeted property-tax revenue of $5,106,044 is 34.25%, the same increase the council adopted. The Hive checked the math.

Where the petition stands (Oct. 1, 2026) Residents are gathering signatures now. FOX 13 reported on Sept. 24 that signatures are due Oct. 8. Weber County's ballot, certified Sept. 3, 2026, already lists Proposition #10. The Hive did not find an official notice declaring the 2026 petition sufficient, an official signature count, or a sponsor list. Those would normally come from the Roy City Recorder and the Weber County Clerk. The Lt. Governor's petition page for the "2026 Roy City Referendum" showed no signers as of Sept. 21; that page may not track paper signatures.

News reports name Nancy Inman, Ty Chaston and Geoffrey Cox as organizers. KSL reported about 1,800 signatures were needed; FOX 13 reported about 1,700. KSL also reported Councilwoman Janel Hulbert said a referendum election would cost the city about $50,000.

4. Last Year's Attempt

This is Roy's second property-tax referendum in two years. In 2025 the council adopted an increase (Resolution 25-24 / Ordinance 25-25, Aug. 19, 2025) to raise $1,423,978. A referendum petition was filed Aug. 25, 2025. It needed 1,743 signatures, 10% of Roy's active voters. The City Recorder declared it insufficient on Oct. 27, 2025.

The city's notice gives two different counts. Its summary says sponsors turned in 714 signatures and 576 were valid; a breakdown table in the same notice lists 831 total and 742 valid. The Lt. Governor's petition page lists 742 signers. Either count is short of 1,743.

Last year's votes were not counted. The 2025 question still appeared on the November ballot. Roy's referendum page says: “Although it was on the November 2025 Election Ballot, any votes cast for the Referendum were not counted.” That is what happened last year; the page does not say how 2026 votes would be handled if this year's petition falls short.

The order of events matters

Before the 2025 petition was counted, the state had already stopped the increase. By Sept. 25, 2025, FOX 13 reported that the State Tax Commission had denied tax increases from 39 Utah entities, and Roy's $1,423,978 was on that list. KSL reported that Roy's was denied because the city had not posted other Weber County entities' proposed increases, as new state guidelines required. City Manager Matt Andrews told KSL the city had followed a Tax Commission checklist that did not list that requirement.

On Oct. 6, 2025, KSL reported that 600 to 700 people had signed and called the ballot question “now likely a moot point.” The petition was declared insufficient three weeks later, on Oct. 27.

What the records show, and what they don't: The 2025 petition was declared insufficient after the state had already denied the increase and news outlets had reported it. The records don't say why more people didn't sign. They do show that by then the state had already stopped the increase, so the count no longer decided whether it took effect.

The Standard-Examiner reported that the 2025 denial left the budget gap behind this year's proposal.

Questions Worth Asking, and Who to Ask

  • Ask the Roy City Recorder's office: when the 2026 referendum petition was filed, how many signatures were turned in and found valid, and whether it has been declared sufficient.
  • Ask the Weber County Clerk/Auditor's elections office: if the petition is found insufficient, whether votes cast on Prop #10 will be counted or reported, as happened in 2025 when they were not counted.
  • Ask Roy City (City Manager's office or Finance): what Roy's adopted 2026 rate is as a decimal, and what changes in the FY2027 budget if voters reject the increase.
  • Ask the Utah State Tax Commission, Property Tax Division: whether Roy's 2026 increase has been approved for this year's tax notices.
  • Ask your state representative: whether the Legislature plans changes to Truth in Taxation or property-tax referendum timing.

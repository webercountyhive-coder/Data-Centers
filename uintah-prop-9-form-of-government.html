Prop 9: Uintah County Voters Decide Whether to Replace Their Commission

A citizen petition qualified by two signatures to ask voters whether to switch from a three-member county commission to a five-member district council and an appointed county manager. Here is what the plan does, what state law says it cannot decide, what the county estimates it would cost, and what each side argues.

New Here? The Short Version

Uintah County is run today by three commissioners elected countywide. Five residents filed a plan to change that, and a petition with 2,103 counted signatures put it on the ballot. If voters say yes to Proposition #9, the county would elect five council members by district in 2028, the council would hire a county manager, the commissioner offices would end, and the change would take effect January 1, 2029. Other county offices, like the sheriff, clerk-auditor and attorney, would still be elected. The county estimates the change would cost about $560,000 a year, a figure that counts only manager and assistant-manager pay and benefits.

1. How It Got on the Ballot

This measure came from residents, not from the county commission. Five sponsors signed the plan: Anna Billings (designated sponsor), Brian Gorum, Dan Dilsaver, Jo Gardner and Nolan Jackson. The county clerk-auditor's office stamped it received on April 20, 2026.

On July 22, 2026, Clerk-Auditor Michael W. Wilkins certified the petition. His letter says his office received 48 signature packets and that the required number of signatures, 2,101, was obtained within 75% of the participation areas. That threshold is 11.5% of the county's 18,272 active voters as of January 1, 2026.

The plan also bars individual council members from pressuring the manager on hiring, firing or purchasing, and bars the manager from raising or giving money for anyone running for county office. Both rules repeat language already in state law. The plan recommends that the five districts start out matching the Uintah School District board districts, but it says it is not drawing the boundaries itself.

3. What the Plan Can't Decide

Two questions come up repeatedly about this measure: how much council members would be paid, and whether they would be part-time. The plan does not answer either one, because state law does not allow it to.

Utah Code § 17-52a-404(3), as amended by H.B. 356 (2025) An optional plan may not: … (b) if the optional plan is proposed after March 20, 2020: (i) specify compensation, including benefits, for any appointed or elected county official; or (ii) specify the full or part-time status of any appointed or elected county official; or (c) … establish, divide, abolish, alter, change, or otherwise attempt to draw boundaries of election districts …

That means council pay, benefits and part-time or full-time status would be set later by the council, under state law, not by the plan voters are approving. Two statements circulating about Prop 9 should be read with that in mind: the For argument's description of council members who "receive limited compensation but no additional benefits," and news coverage describing a "part-time" council. Neither appears in the plan, and the plan could not legally guarantee either.

The same 2025 bill requires smaller counties (third through sixth class) that use the council-manager form to elect each council member from a single district. That is why the plan uses districts rather than at-large seats.

A note on section numbers H.B. 356 amended Chapter 52a of Title 17. The optional plan and the county cite the same provisions as Chapter 62 (§§ 17-62-303, -403, -404), the current numbering after Title 17 was reorganized. The Hive read the 2025 enrolled bill text; it did not separately confirm the current wording under the new numbering.

4. The County's Cost Estimate

State law requires a fiscal impact statement for this kind of measure. The one in the voter pamphlet is signed by Clerk-Auditor Michael W. Wilkins and Deputy Civil County Attorney Loren W. Anderson.

$560,000Estimated annual expense
$560,000Shown as a property-tax increase
$0New public debt
$10,000Cost to print and mail the pamphlet

The statement says the $560,000 is only the average total wages and estimated benefits of a county manager and an assistant county manager in three comparable council-manager counties. It does not name the three counties. It lists no cost savings, and it does not estimate any difference between what three commissioners cost now and what five council members would cost. As Section 3 explains, the plan itself sets no council pay. The statement found no significant effect on vested property rights, other county ordinances or county legal liability.

5. What Each Side Argues

These are summaries of the written arguments printed in the county's voter pamphlet. The county notes that the arguments are the authors' opinions and were not edited by the county. Contact details printed in the pamphlet are not reproduced here.

For Prop 9

  • A five-member council elected by district gives every part of the county its own representative who lives there, and spreads lawmaking power among more elected people.
  • The roles become clearer: the elected council makes law, and a professional manager handles daily administration and answers to the council.
  • The form is approved by state law and already used in Summit, Tooele and Wasatch counties, and by many cities and water boards.
  • In rebuttal: the manager is hired, supervised and removable by elected council members, so calling the manager unaccountable misreads the plan; and residents in areas with no commissioner living there would gain a representative.
Signed: Anna Billings (designated sponsor), Brian Gorum, Dan Dilsaver, Jo Gardner, Nolan Jackson

Against Prop 9

  • Voters now help choose all three commissioners. Under districts, each voter would choose one of five, while the other four would still vote on that voter's taxes, roads and public lands.
  • District races narrow the field. If only one candidate files in a district, that is the voter's only choice, even if a stronger candidate lives across the line.
  • Moving executive authority from three elected commissioners to one appointed manager concentrates power in a person voters cannot remove; only the council can.
  • Three commissioners bring different perspectives and divide the work of advocating for the county at the Legislature, which matters for a county shaped by energy, agriculture and public lands.
  • In rebuttal: the plan cannot set council pay or schedules, so limited compensation is not guaranteed; and the opponents state that all three Utah counties that adopted this form saw costs rise.
Signed: Mike McKee, Kevin Van Tassell, Jeff Merrell, Darlene Burns, Kim McDonald (names as printed, "Mike Mckee")

Questions Worth Asking, and Who to Ask

  • Ask the Uintah County Clerk-Auditor's office: which three counties the $560,000 manager and assistant-manager average comes from, and what the three commissioners' salaries and benefits cost the county today.
  • Ask the Uintah County Clerk-Auditor's office: for the sponsors' campaign financial reports listed on the county's referenda page, and whether any group opposing Prop 9 has filed one.
  • Ask the Uintah County Attorney's office: if Prop 9 passes, who draws the five district lines, when, and whether the public gets a hearing on the map.
  • Ask the Uintah County Commission: whether the commission has taken any official position on Prop 9, and where that is recorded.
  • Ask your state senator (Sen. Ron Winterton, District 20) or state representative: why H.B. 356 (2025) requires district-only elections for council-manager counties of Uintah's size.
  • Check the hearing record yourself: audio for all four August hearings and minutes for three are posted on uintah.gov.

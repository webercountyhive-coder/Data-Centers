Data Center Entry and U.S. Electricity Prices

The full academic study behind Case File 4's summary slides. The headline finding: data centers do raise electricity prices — but the effect is concentrated almost entirely on electric cooperatives and municipal utilities, not investor-owned utilities like Rocky Mountain Power.

Authors: Angela Ryu & Shon R. Hiatt, USC Marshall
Published: May 22, 2026
Submitted to: EDWS Interim Committee, Aug. 19, 2026

Locational Marginal Price
The wholesale price of electricity at a specific point on the grid at a specific time — made up of an energy cost, a congestion cost, and a loss cost added together.

Regional Transmission Organization
A nonprofit that runs the wholesale power grid across a multi-state region and sets prices. PJM covers the Mid-Atlantic and parts of the Midwest (Pennsylvania to Illinois, plus D.C.). MISO covers the Midwest and parts of the South, from the Dakotas down to Louisiana. ERCOT is Texas; SPP is the Great Plains. Utah isn't in any of them, and isn't in this study's price data.

Extended Day-Ahead Market
A shared wholesale power-trading market, run by CAISO, that utilities across the West can join voluntarily. Instead of scheduling power only within its own territory, a member utility gets access to the whole shared market a day in advance. RMP is joining in May 2026.
Why this matters here

This whole study's headline result is a story about ownership type: IOU, cooperative, or municipal. RMP — the utility serving most of Utah — is an IOU. Everything below is really answering one question: does data center growth raise prices for RMP customers specifically, or for someone else?

The one-paragraph answer

Data center entry raises wholesale electricity prices, consistently and with strong statistical confidence. But at the retail level — the number on your actual bill — the effect the authors can detect with confidence is concentrated in cooperative and municipal utility territory. For investor-owned utilities, which serve 68% of U.S. customers and include Rocky Mountain Power, the retail effect is not statistically significant.

01

Wholesale prices: the effect is real and well-documented

The study traces price effects at three geographic scales, from the transmission node up to the household bill.

+$2.30/MWh
Node-level congestion (CAISO)
+$3.44/MWh
Zone-level total price (non-CAISO)
+$5.43/MWh
Zone-level energy component

At the node level (California's grid specifically), a new data center raises nearby congestion costs — consistent with the facility straining a specific point on the local transmission system. At the zone level, across PJM, ERCOT, MISO, and SPP, the effect shows up almost entirely in the energy component of price: additional demand forces the grid operator to dispatch more expensive power plants to meet it, and that raises the price for everyone drawing from that zone.

"The wholesale price pressure documented here is consistent with a tightening supply-demand balance in affected wholesale zones." Executive Summary

— Section 4.1–4.2, "Wholesale Node-Level Effects" and "Wholesale Zone-Level Effects"

02

Retail prices: the effect depends entirely on who your utility is

This is the section that matters most for Utah. When operating data center capacity in a utility's territory doubles, the study finds an average 0.65% increase in residential electricity prices — roughly $1.04–$1.10 per megawatt-hour at typical rates. But that average hides a sharp split by ownership type:

Ownership typeEffect when capacity doublesStatistically significant?
Cooperative+0.42% (~$0.67–$0.71/MWh)Yes
Municipal+0.63% (~$1.01–$1.07/MWh)Yes
Investor-owned (RMP's type)+0.06% (~$0.07–$0.08/MWh)No

Why the split happens

The authors' explanation: IOUs are larger, have more sophisticated procurement (long-term contracts, hedging), and have more market exposure to spread the cost around. Cooperatives and municipals tend to be smaller utilities more directly exposed to zone-clearing wholesale prices — even when they aren't the ones hosting the data center themselves.

03

What the study can't yet tell us

The authors are explicit about their own limits. Two matter for reading this docket honestly:

  • This is a study of recent entry effects under historically adequate reserve margins — it does not model what happens as the currently announced data center pipeline actually gets built out nationally.
  • The forward-looking reliability data (NERC's 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment) projects the steepest reserve margin declines in PJM (the Mid-Atlantic grid, Pennsylvania to Illinois) and MISO (the Midwest/South grid, the Dakotas to Louisiana) — not the regions Utah's grid operator, Rocky Mountain Power, sits in. This study's price findings are strongest exactly where Utah isn't.
The reserve margin numbers, if you want them

PJM's Anticipated Reserve Margin falls from 29.7% (2026) to 13.9% (2030) while its Reference Margin Level rises from 18.6% to 26.3% — a projected 12.4-point deficit. MISO reaches a 4.2-point deficit by 2030. ERCOT and SPP are projected to remain above their reference levels through the period, though ERCOT's adequacy depends heavily on a demand-response expansion tied to Texas's large-load curtailment authority.

04

Reading it against Utah specifically

The RMP question

Rocky Mountain Power, which serves most of Utah, is an investor-owned utility — the exact category this study finds no statistically significant retail price effect for. Taken at face value, this national study would suggest Utah residential ratepayers are among the least likely, by this methodology, to see a measurable data-center-driven bill increase from wholesale pass-through.

That's a real finding, and it cuts against the instinct to assume data centers are straightforwardly raising Utah bills through this specific channel. But it comes with real limits worth holding onto: Utah isn't in any of the four RTOs this study's wholesale price data comes from, so none of the wholesale price effects in Section 01 were measured using Utah data at all. And a null result for IOU retail prices nationally doesn't rule out the other cost channels this docket has already documented in Utah specifically — the property tax mechanisms in Case File 4, or the closed private generation pathway in Case File 1 that removes a project from PSC rate review (and this study's retail price panel) entirely.

This isn't a settled question — it's an active fight at the PSC and in the press

Rocky Mountain Power (PacifiCorp) operates the same multi-state system across Utah, Wyoming, Oregon, Washington, Idaho, and California. In May 2026, RMP publicly denied — in a statement responding to press questions in Wyoming — that an 8.8% rate hike request there was driven by data centers. The fact that RMP had to issue that denial at all shows this is a live, contested public narrative, not a resolved one.

There's also a real, currently active party at Utah's own PSC: the Utah Large Customer Group (UTLCG), an intervenor in RMP's 2024–2025 Utah rate case (Docket 24-035-04) that negotiated a partial settlement with RMP specifically over data reporting for large-load customers. Separately, the PSC's own investigation into a large-load flexible tariff for RMP's territory — ordered by SB 132 — is still in progress with no public outcome yet.

Worth noting on the other side: intervenor testimony from Utah Clean Energy in that same 2024–2025 rate case attributes most of RMP's proposed increase to wildfire mitigation costs and fuel prices, not data centers — which is broadly consistent with this study's finding. And RMP is scheduled to enter CAISO's day-ahead wholesale market (EDAM, the Extended Day-Ahead Market — a shared power-trading system across Western utilities, one day in advance) in May 2026. That's the same kind of wholesale market this study's node-level findings were measured in, just in California specifically; RMP joining it is a structural change the study's historical Utah data wouldn't have captured, and it could change Utah's wholesale price exposure going forward.

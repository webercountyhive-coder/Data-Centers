INTRODUCTION

What This Page Is About

Meta runs a large data center in Eagle Mountain, in Utah County. To power its expansion, a natural gas power plant is planned on Meta's land. Williams will own it, and Meta Platforms, Inc. guarantees the contract. Meta's Sept. 14, 2026 announcement did not mention the plant.

This is Part 2. It lays out what public filings show about the plant, its contracts, its air permit, how it may be taxed and who decides what happens next. Part 1 covers the campus itself: the 2018 deal, the tax breaks, jobs, water and 2024 energy use.

Why it matters outside Eagle Mountain: the state law this plant is filed under (S.B. 132) applies everywhere in Utah. The tax, air and water decisions made here will set the pattern for the next data center, including the ones proposed in northern Utah, like Stratos in Box Elder County.

How it was made: everything comes from public records: city, state and utility filings, campaign finance reports and published news. No one was asked for comment. Anything not yet decided is marked that way.

Five Things the Records Show

IN PLAIN WORDSMeta is getting a private gas power plant built next to its data center. Most of the public story is about Meta's clean energy, but city and state paperwork show the gas plant too.

  1. A gas plant is being permitted to power Meta's expansion. Williams told Eagle Mountain's Planning Commission it is a "286-megawatt generating facility" with "a half-mile overhead power delivery line to Meta's facility," "behind the meter, separated from Rocky Mountain Power" (wired straight to Meta, never using the public power lines).
  2. Meta guarantees the deal, and it was signed early. The power contract names "Meta Platforms, Inc., as Guarantor" (Meta promises to pay if its buyer company doesn't) and dates to Sept. 30, 2025, nearly four months before the plant first appeared on a public city agenda. The applicant asked state regulators to keep the customer's name confidential; the redacted contract still shows it.
  3. Meta's own announcement doesn't mention it. Meta's Sept. 14 post describes renewables and batteries and says the campus is "matched with 100% clean and renewable energy."
  4. The air permit and the state approval are both pending. The state Division of Air Quality has a notice of intent under review. The Public Service Commission has put the private power contract on hold because the application is incomplete.
  5. The city agreed to 40 years of limits on itself. In the draft agreement, the city won't support new taxes aimed at the plant "or the data center industry," won't apply future rule changes to it, and must alert the company to any public-records request about it. Only the company can end the deal early. The payment to the city is left blank.

WHAT META IS DOING, AND NOT DOING

From Meta's Words and the Filings

IN PLAIN WORDSHere is what the documents show Meta doing, and what they show it not doing or not saying.

Each item is from a public document linked in Sources. Meta is not the applicant in the state case; the power contract runs through Williams entities, with Meta as guarantor.

What the records show Meta doing

  • Guaranteeing a gas power contract. "Parent Guaranty dated October 8, 2025, with Meta Platforms, Inc., as Guarantor."
  • Hosting the plant on its campus. The city says natural gas power is "a permitted accessory use" under the Stadion (Meta) development agreement.
  • Buying renewables through the utility. Meta says it has "added more than 1.7 GW of clean and renewable energy and 600MWh of battery storage" with retail partners.
  • Receiving tax breaks. The 2018 Eagle Mountain package gave 100% relief on personal property (servers and equipment) and 80% on real property (land and buildings) for 40 years, as reported by the Deseret News. The Sweetwater #4 CRA plan requests $778.5 million in tax increment.
  • Funding community projects. Meta lists $450,000 for an astronomy park, $600,000 for school land, $1.5 million to the Lake Mountain Education Foundation and "$50+ million in local infrastructure improvements."
  • Spending in Utah elections. Its PAC (a political committee that spends in elections), Forge the Future Project, spent $251,446.36 on two 2026 primaries (Tami Tran and Logan Monson).

What the records show Meta not doing

  • Not mentioning the gas plant in its Sept. 14, 2026 Eagle Mountain announcement.
  • Not appearing by name as the buyer in the state filing. The applicant asked to keep "the name of the customer" confidential.
  • Not a party to the Public Service Commission case. Williams' Will-Power UT is the applicant; Rocky Mountain Power intervened.
  • Not relying on the public grid for backup. The filing says "Rocky Mountain Power will not provide backup power in the event of an outage."
  • Not paying the plant's taxes directly. The contract puts "all Taxes… with respect to the Generation Facility" on the seller, Williams.

The fair reading

"Matched with 100% clean and renewable energy" describes an accounting match: Meta buys as much renewable power over a year as the campus uses. It does not mean every hour of power it uses is clean. It can be true at the same time a gas plant serves part of the campus. The records don't show how much of the expansion's power will come from gas versus the grid, and Meta's announcement doesn't say.

Why "self-generate" matters: state law says a plant making power "solely for the producer's own use" is not a public utility, which bears on who assesses its property tax. H.B. 76 counts a data center's power equipment as part of the data center when the generation is "by the operator." The contract's wording could matter for both. Neither question has been decided.

THE AIR PERMIT

Applied For, Not Yet Public for Comment

IN PLAIN WORDSAnything that pollutes the air needs a state permit. Williams has applied. The public gets 30 days to comment once the state posts a draft.

Williams' filing says Will-Power "has applied for the requisite Power Gen Air Permit." A Division of Air Quality modeling memo dated April 23, 2026 (DAQE-MN163220001A-26) reviews the application and places the site in "a marginal nonattainment area" for ozone (the area already has too much smog, in the least-severe category) and a maintenance area for PM10 (it used to have too much dust and is being watched). It states: "This is not a Major Prevention of Significant Deterioration (PSD) Source," meaning it is below the size that triggers the strictest federal permit.

Williams' own summary says the plant "will not cause or contribute to any new air quality violations." The emissions figures in that exhibit are redacted.

When DAQ publishes its intent to approve, a 30-day public comment period opens. At two other Utah data center gas plants, Novva and Joule, DAQ recorded "No public comments" on the final permits.

The First Private Power Contract Utah Has Reviewed

IN PLAIN WORDSA 2025 law lets big users buy power from their own private plant. The state must approve each deal. This one is stuck because a required form is missing.

S.B. 132 (2025) lets customers of 100 megawatts or more buy power from a private generator that is completely separate from the utility. The PSC must approve the contract and confirm ratepayers bear none of the cost.

TAXES

Who Taxes a Plant That Serves One Customer?

IN PLAIN WORDSNormal power plants are taxed by the state. A plant serving only one company might be taxed by the county instead. Nobody has decided which applies here.

Utah's Tax Commission centrally assesses (the state sets the value of) "all property of public utilities." A utility, by law, serves "the public generally," and a plant generating "solely for the producer's own use" is not an electrical corporation. S.B. 132 exempts closed private systems from regulation "as a public utility." Read together, a plant like Aquila may fall to the Utah County assessor instead of the Tax Commission. No ruling or Tax Commission guidance found says either way; the Gardner Institute's August 2026 brief says power plants "may be centrally assessed."

The contract makes Williams responsible for the plant's taxes and says both sides "shall reasonably cooperate to minimize each Party's Taxes." The city's draft agreement bars it from supporting any new tax aimed solely at the plant "or the data center industry," and lets the company "protest, oppose and vote against any and all Taxes."

An AI-generated summary of a Nov. 26, 2025 Utah County Commission meeting quotes staff saying the power facility adds about $350 million in value and sits outside the CRA boundary. That has not been checked against the official minutes and is not used here as fact.

CONCLUSION

What We Know, and What We Don't

IN PLAIN WORDSThis is the whole page in two lists: what the records clearly show, and what is still undecided.

WHAT THE RECORDS SHOW

Known

  • A Williams gas plant of up to 286 MW is filed for Meta's Eagle Mountain land, separate from Rocky Mountain Power.
  • Meta Platforms, Inc. guarantees the power contract.
  • Meta's Sept. 14, 2026 announcement did not mention the plant.
  • The city's draft agreement runs 40 years, allows 75 dBA of noise and freezes the rules; the company can end it at any time.
  • Meta's campus already gets large property tax breaks, and more tax growth is requested for the area's redevelopment fund.
  • The campus used about 2.9% of Utah's electricity in 2024, but little water for its size (Meta's figures).
  • Meta's PAC spent $251,446.36 in two 2026 legislative primaries.
  • The filings followed the required steps so far: the Planning Commission heard it in public, and the PSC flagged missing paperwork.
STILL UNDECIDED

Not known yet

  • Whether the state approves the power contract, or drops the utility sign-off rule.
  • How much the plant may pollute, and whether the public can comment.
  • What the signed city agreement says, including the payment.
  • Who values the plant for taxes, and how much it will pay.
  • How much water the new buildings use.
  • What the 2027 data center bill and the new committees will do.
  • Why Meta's PAC chose the candidates it did. The filings don't say.

Bottom line: nothing in these records shows a rule was broken. What they show is a big energy decision being made in pieces, across a city, two state agencies and the Legislature, while the company's public announcement talks only about clean energy.

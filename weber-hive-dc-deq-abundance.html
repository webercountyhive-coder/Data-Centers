Why this matters here

DEQ's whole point in this presentation is that these approvals don't all happen together, aren't all required for every project, and don't all come with a protest period or public comment window. Knowing which agency does what — and which ones come with public input attached — is what makes the approval matrix below readable.

How a data center actually gets approved in Utah

This is the core of DEQ's presentation and the most useful reference in this whole docket — read it in full, not just skimmed.

01

DEQ's seven key observations

Slide 4 of the deck lists these as the department's own framing, verbatim in substance:

  1. No single agency approves a data center. Responsibilities are distributed across multiple state and local processes.
  2. Local governments are often the entry point. Most projects begin as local land-use or development applications before any state environmental permit is completed.
  3. Agency involvement depends on project design. Water demand, power supply, emissions sources, and infrastructure choices determine which approvals apply — meaning two data centers can face entirely different regulatory paths.
  4. Many reviews occur concurrently. Utility planning, environmental permitting, engineering design, and local review frequently overlap rather than proceeding in sequence.
  5. Public participation varies by approval. Some reviews include formal notice-and-comment; others are purely administrative or technical, with no public comment period at all.
  6. Recent legislation added targeted requirements. HB 76 added water notice/reporting for certain large data centers; SB 132 created the large electric service framework.
  7. Compliance continues after construction. Several approvals carry ongoing monitoring, reporting, inspection, or renewal obligations.

— Slide 4, "Key observations"

Worth noting

Observation 5 — that public participation "varies by approval" — is doing a lot of quiet work. In practice it means a data center's water rights or PSC-approved large-load contract can move forward with a formal comment period, while its air-emissions permit, building permit, or private generation contract can clear without one. DEQ states this plainly; it isn't hidden. But it's also the single sentence that best explains why community opposition efforts (moratoriums, the county-level pushback covered in Case File 4) end up aimed at zoning and land use — that's often the only stage where public comment is guaranteed.

02

The six-phase development process

DEQ frames this explicitly as "an organizing framework rather than statutory stages" — in practice, phases overlap and the order varies by project.

Phase 1
Planning & site evaluation
Site, load, cooling, water, utility, and land constraints assessed.
Phase 2
Local review & initial evaluation
Zoning, entitlement, local infrastructure, HB 76 notice if triggered.
Phase 3
Resource planning & coordination
Electric service, water supply, wastewater, generation, transmission.
Phase 4
Permitting & state reviews
Air, water quality, water rights, drinking water, PSC where applicable.
Phase 5
Construction & implementation
Building, fire, civil work, utility upgrades, facility construction.
Phase 6
Operations & compliance
Monitoring, reporting, renewals, inspections, annual water reporting.

Additional permits worth knowing about

Slide 6 lists a second tier of possible approvals that "may apply depending on project location, design, and operational characteristics" — most notably, that wetland and stream-alteration permits from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers typically do include public notice, even when the primary state/local approvals don't. Historic and cultural resource review through the Utah SHPO is a consultation process, not a public comment period. Hazardous materials, fuel storage, and spill-prevention requirements (local fire authority, EPA) carry no public review at all.

— Slide 6, "Additional permits and approval considerations"

04

What this means, read against the other decks

Cross-reference

This deck is the clearest confirmation, from inside state government, of the structural point in Case File 1: which approvals a data center faces is a design choice the developer makes, not a fixed rule the state applies equally to everyone. A closed private generation system under SB 132 sidesteps the PSC review row in this matrix entirely — DEQ's own chart confirms that PSC involvement is conditional ("when using qualifying large-load electric service arrangements"), not universal.

DEQ presents this fragmentation neutrally — as a factual description of how Utah's regulatory system is built, not as a criticism of it. Whether that fragmentation is a feature (flexibility, faster permitting) or a gap (inconsistent public input, easy-to-avoid review) is exactly the kind of question this docket exists to let readers weigh against the other four case files.

# The National Math Behind Utah's Data Center Bets — and Who Works There

**A global consulting firm says the AI building boom is running ahead of the money needed to pay for it. Utah's proposed campuses are among the largest in the country. Here is what the public record shows about their size, the risk, and the people who will work inside them.**

*The Weber County Hive — We report public records. You decide.*

---

## The national math

On Sept. 29, 2026, Bain & Company published a report on what it will take to pay for the artificial intelligence building boom. Its main points, in plain terms:

- The five biggest tech spenders (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta and Oracle) could spend about **$780 billion** on buildout in 2026, nearly five times what they spent three years earlier.
- The largest AI data centers today are near **1 gigawatt**. Bain expects 2-gigawatt facilities by 2027 and **9-gigawatt campuses** by about 2030.
- To support that spending, Bain estimates the AI industry would need about **$6 trillion a year** in revenue by 2031. Today's consumer and business AI products could bring in **$1.2 trillion to $1.8 trillion**. That leaves about **$4.2 trillion** that, in Bain's words, must come from new sources: AI search advertising, self-driving vehicles, robotics and products not yet invented.
- Bain's own conclusion is that the buildings are going up ahead of demand, and whether the new uses arrive in time to pay for them is an open question.

Bain is a consulting firm that sells AI services, and its numbers are estimates built on its own assumptions, including that spending equals about 25% of industry revenue. They are a forecast, not a finding.

## Where Utah sits on that curve

Bain describes 9 gigawatts as the size of the very largest campuses expected by the end of the decade. Utah already has proposals at that scale.

| Project | Location | Size listed | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creekstone Delta Gigasite | Delta, Millard County | more than 10 GW | Under construction |
| Stratos Project | Hansel Valley, Box Elder County | 7.5 GW total (9 GW at full build-out per the Sierra Club) | Proposed |
| Joule campus | Holden, Millard County | 4 GW | Under construction |
| Tract Pole Canyon | Eagle Mountain | 1.7 GW | Proposed |
| Antelope Data Campus / Red Butte | Iron County | 1.5 GW each | Proposed |

*Source: Salt Lake Tribune data center map, Sept. 25, 2026.*

The scale of change is large by any measure. The Governor's Office of Economic Development (GOED), presenting to the Legislature's Economic Development and Workforce Services Interim Committee on Aug. 19, 2026, reported that Utah has **48 operating data centers with more than 1 gigawatt of capacity**, and that projects under construction will add **2.6 gigawatts**. The Tribune's table, which lists each proposed campus at its full planned size, adds up to roughly **29 gigawatts** of proposed and under-construction projects. Those two figures measure different things (current construction phases versus full build-out), but both point the same direction.

## Who carries the risk if the demand doesn't arrive

Bain is writing for investors. In Utah, some of the tools used to build these projects are public ones.

- **Stratos.** The Master Development Agreement allows the developer to ask MIDA to form public infrastructure districts that could use the developer's 80% share of payments as security for bonds (§13). Neither party may terminate the agreement (§15). The agreement is still a draft and unsigned, according to MIDA's own document page.
- **Tooele Valley.** The Utah Inland Port Authority approved a $4.875 million short-term loan to Zenith Bolinder, the data center developer, with listed uses that include a power plant. That loan has been paid off.
- **Sales tax.** Utah's data center sales tax exemption (S.B. 114, 2020) applies regardless of how the project performs.

None of this shows anyone did anything wrong. It shows where public money and public tools sit if a national forecast turns out to be too optimistic.

## Who works there?

This is the part of the story most people ask about first.

| Project | Construction workers | Permanent jobs after | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meta, Eagle Mountain | 1,200+ at peak | 200 "supported"; a few dozen employees to run it (2021). Stated as 300+ for the expanded campus (2026) | Meta, via Deseret News |
| QTS, Eagle Mountain ($6 billion) | 2,000+ | about 100, counting employees, tenants and contractors | QTS |
| Stratos, Box Elder County | 4,000 (Tribune, May 8, 2026) to 10,000 (Forbes, June 9, 2026) | 2,000 claimed; no tenant has been announced | Project spokespeople |
| Creekstone, Delta | "thousands" | 106 | GOED presentation, Aug. 19, 2026 |
| Joule, Holden | not disclosed | not disclosed | — |
| **All of Utah** | — | **2,000 to 3,250 by 2030** | GOED presentation, Aug. 19, 2026 |

A few points stand out:

- **The state's own statewide forecast is 2,000 to 3,250 permanent data center jobs by 2030**, set against tens of gigawatts of proposed projects.
- **Stratos's figures have varied.** Project spokespeople have given construction estimates ranging from 4,000 to 10,000. O'Leary Ventures CEO Paul Palandjian later described about 4,000 workers over 10 to 15 years and said the numbers change daily. A University of Southern California analysis cited by TechRepublic estimated the permanent figure closer to 1,350.
- **Nationally, the permanent number is small by design.** A Brookings study of about 1,500 U.S. data centers (Bahar and Wright, updated Aug. 10, 2026) found roughly 100 to 200 jobs created per county that receives its first major data center. Wages did not change. Home prices rose 2% to 5%.
- **Forecasts get revised.** Georgia's state audit originally projected 5,471 data center operations jobs. A revised version in January 2026 cut that to 1,641, and construction jobs from 28,350 to 8,505. Georgia's exemption cost $474 million in the fiscal year ending July 2025.

## Are the workers local?

**No Utah record reviewed for this piece reports how many data center workers are local, or even Utah residents.**

- **The main state tax break requires no jobs at all.** S.B. 114 (2020), with Sen. Kirk Cullimore as primary sponsor and Rep. Mike Schultz as House sponsor, set no jobs, wage or investment requirement. It passed unanimously, and its fiscal note listed the cost as "unknown." In July 2026, State Auditor Tina Cannon said Utah still has no system to calculate what it costs.
- **The state's jobs-based incentive measures pay, not residence.** GOED's Aug. 19 slides list wage standards for EDTIF (110% of the county's average wage) and REDTIF (100%). They list no residency standard. The one data center GOED has given a state credit to, Creekstone near Delta, gets 50% of new state tax revenue back for 20 years on a projected $17 billion+ investment and 106 permanent jobs, according to GOED's slide 9.
- **Stratos's own documents plan for workers who don't live nearby.** The draft interlocal agreement bans housing in the project area except "temporary housing for construction workers." The Development Standards allow lodging for employees and RV parks in the mixed-use zone. Hotel rooms are capped at 150 for the entire 40,000 acres. A project spokesperson told the Tribune construction workers would be housed on site.
- **Stratos's job reports can be sealed.** The draft interlocal agreement requires yearly employee counts to Box Elder County, but allows them to be marked confidential under Utah's records law.
- **The contractors' trade group expects outside hiring.** An Associated General Contractors of America analyst told KUTV that contractors often turn to outside firms and unions because local labor pools are small in places like Box Elder County.
- **Millard County had 13,437 residents in 2023.** Two of the largest proposed campuses in the country are planned there.

## When jobs are the condition

Utah does have an incentive tied to jobs. Tyson Foods received a state tax-credit agreement worth up to $5.26 million for its Eagle Mountain plant, paid after performance. The plant is closing Oct. 12, 2026, five years into a ten-year agreement, with 723 jobs lost. Credits stop when the jobs stop. Whether any credits already paid can be recovered has not been established in the records reviewed.

## The other side

Jobs are not the only thing a community gets, and the public record includes reasons officials support these projects.

- **Some counties prefer few jobs.** Millard County Commissioner Bill Wright told Utah Business that data centers are preferable to an employer that brings hundreds of new residents who need houses and schools.
- **Utah companies get work.** QTS built with Utah-based Layton Construction and steel suppliers from Cedar City.
- **The tax money is real.** Former Eagle Mountain Mayor Tom Westmoreland said the Meta land brought in "$35 annually" in taxes before the project. Current Mayor Jared Gray told the Tribune data centers are "literally what funds our general fund."

## What the record shows

Bain's report says the national buildout is running ahead of proven demand. Utah's proposed campuses are sized at the top of that curve. The state's own forecast is a few thousand permanent jobs statewide. And no Utah record reviewed shows how many of those jobs, or the thousands of construction jobs before them, go to Utahns.

The National Math Behind Utah's Data Center Bets — and Who Works There

A global consulting firm says the AI building boom is running ahead of the money needed to pay for it. Utah's proposed campuses are among the largest in the country. Here is what the public record shows about their size, the risk, and the people who will work inside them.

The national math

On Sept. 29, 2026, Bain & Company published a report on what it will take to pay for the artificial intelligence building boom. In plain terms:

  • The five biggest tech spenders (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta and Oracle) could spend about $780 billion on buildout in 2026, nearly five times what they spent three years earlier.
  • The largest AI data centers today are near 1 gigawatt. Bain expects 2-gigawatt facilities by 2027 and 9-gigawatt campuses by about 2030.
  • To support that spending, Bain estimates the industry needs about $6 trillion a year in revenue by 2031. Today's products could bring in $1.2 to $1.8 trillion.

Read this as a forecast

Bain is a consulting firm that sells AI services. Its numbers are estimates built on its own assumptions, including that spending equals about 25% of industry revenue. Bain's own conclusion is that the buildings are going up ahead of demand, and whether new uses arrive in time to pay for them is an open question.

Where Utah sits on that curve

Bain describes 9 gigawatts as the size of the very largest campuses expected by the end of the decade. Utah already has proposals at that scale.

The Governor's Office of Economic Development (GOED), presenting to the Legislature's Economic Development and Workforce Services Interim Committee on Aug. 19, 2026, reported that Utah has 48 operating data centers with more than 1 gigawatt of capacity, and that projects under construction will add 2.6 gigawatts. The Tribune's table, which lists each campus at its full planned size, adds up to roughly 29 gigawatts. Those figures measure different things (current construction phases versus full build-out), but both point the same direction.

Who carries the risk if demand doesn't arrive

Bain is writing for investors. In Utah, some of the tools used to build these projects are public ones.

  • Stratos. The Master Development Agreement allows the developer to ask MIDA to form public infrastructure districts that could use the developer's 80% share of payments as security for bonds (§13). Neither party may terminate the agreement (§15). MIDA's own document page says the agreement is still a draft and unsigned. Stratos case file →
  • Tooele Valley. The Utah Inland Port Authority approved a $4.875 million short-term loan to Zenith Bolinder, the data center developer, with listed uses that include a power plant. That loan has been paid off.
  • Sales tax. Utah's data center sales tax exemption (S.B. 114) applies regardless of how the project performs.

None of this shows anyone did anything wrong. It shows where public money and public tools sit if a national forecast turns out to be too optimistic.

Who works there?

Stratos shows the lowest construction figure given (4,000). Another spokesperson figure was 10,000. Stratos's 2,000 permanent jobs is a claim; no tenant has been announced.

  • Stratos's figures have varied. The Tribune reported 4,000 construction jobs on May 8, 2026; Forbes reported 10,000 on June 9. O'Leary Ventures CEO Paul Palandjian later described about 4,000 workers over 10 to 15 years and said the numbers change daily. A University of Southern California analysis cited by TechRepublic estimated the permanent figure closer to 1,350.
  • Nationally, the permanent number is small by design. A Brookings study of about 1,500 U.S. data centers (Bahar and Wright, updated Aug. 10, 2026) found roughly 100 to 200 jobs created per county that gets its first major data center. Wages did not change. Home prices rose 2% to 5%.
  • Forecasts get revised. Georgia's state audit first projected 5,471 data center operations jobs. A January 2026 revision cut that to 1,641, and construction jobs from 28,350 to 8,505. Georgia's exemption cost $474 million in the fiscal year ending July 2025.

Are the workers local?

The short answer

No Utah record reviewed for this case file reports how many data center workers are local, or even Utah residents.

  • The main state tax break requires no jobs at all. S.B. 114 (2020), with Sen. Kirk Cullimore as primary sponsor and Rep. Mike Schultz as House sponsor, set no jobs, wage or investment requirement. It passed unanimously, and its fiscal note listed the cost as "unknown." In July 2026, State Auditor Tina Cannon said Utah still has no system to calculate what it costs.
  • The state's jobs-based incentive measures pay, not residence. GOED's Aug. 19 slides list wage standards for EDTIF (110% of the county's average wage) and REDTIF (100%). They list no residency standard. The one data center GOED has given a state credit to, Creekstone near Delta, gets 50% of new state tax revenue back for 20 years on a projected $17 billion+ investment and 106 permanent jobs, according to GOED's slide 9. GOED case file →
  • Stratos's documents plan for workers who don't live nearby. The draft interlocal agreement bans housing in the project area except "temporary housing for construction workers." The Development Standards allow lodging for employees and RV parks in the mixed-use zone, and hotel rooms are capped at 150 for the entire 40,000 acres. A project spokesperson told the Tribune construction workers would be housed on site.
  • The contractors' trade group expects outside hiring. An Associated General Contractors of America analyst told KUTV that contractors often turn to outside firms and unions because local labor pools are small in places like Box Elder County.
  • Millard County had 13,437 residents in 2023. Two of the largest proposed campuses in the country are planned there.

Utah does have an incentive tied to jobs. Tyson Foods received a state tax-credit agreement worth up to $5.26 million for its Eagle Mountain plant. The plant is closing five years into a ten-year agreement, with 723 jobs lost. Credits stop when the jobs stop. Whether any credits already paid can be recovered has not been established in the records reviewed.

The other side

  • Some counties prefer few jobs. Millard County Commissioner Bill Wright told Utah Business that data centers are preferable to an employer that brings hundreds of new residents who need houses and schools.
  • Utah companies get work. QTS built with Utah-based Layton Construction and steel suppliers from Cedar City.
  • The tax money is real. Former Eagle Mountain Mayor Tom Westmoreland said the Meta land brought in "$35 annually" in taxes before the project. Current Mayor Jared Gray told the Tribune data centers are "literally what funds our general fund." Meta case file →

What the record shows

Bain's report says the national buildout is running ahead of proven demand. Utah's proposed campuses are sized at the top of that curve. The state's own forecast is a few thousand permanent jobs statewide. And no Utah record reviewed shows how many of those jobs, or the thousands of construction jobs before them, go to Utahns.
